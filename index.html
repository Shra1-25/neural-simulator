What If, and Why

A neural simulator that learns physics to predict, infer, intervene and explain.

Shravan Chaudhari · Johns Hopkins University

Preliminary examples (ongoing work) · 9 November 2026

1Predict 2Intervene 3Infer 4Explain

A rescue robot must clear rubble without bringing it down on a trapped person (the red marker). It knows each piece's shape and position, but not how heavy it is. Each example is a held-out pile, simulated by the physics engine (PhysX in NVIDIA Isaac Sim) and by our neural simulator, which never saw it. Hover over a clip, or tap it, to play it.

1

Predict

Will this pull bring the pile down?

Pulling slab A brings the pile down; pulling slab B leaves it standing, in the engine and in the simulator. Without knowing the masses, the simulator gives pull A an 82% chance of a collapse and pull B 4%.

2

Intervene

The pile starts to slide. Brace, or let go?

A pulled block starts a slide. From that moment, the simulator continues the motion for each choice: letting go, the pile keeps sliding; bracing the chunk beside it, the pile stops, as in the engine.

3

Infer

A firm pull barely moves this slab. Is it heavy?

The slab moved 3 cm. The simulator predicts it would move 2 m if it weighed what it looks like, and 6 cm if it were six times heavier. So it is heavy (it is 5.4 times heavier than it looks).

4

Explain

The pile came down. Would another pull have been safe?

Pulling a supporting brick (A) brought the pile down. Had the robot pulled a loose brick (B) instead, the simulator says the pile would have stayed up, and the engine agrees.

The simulator answers from what the robot knows (shapes, positions, masses guessed from looks), drawing several guesses about the hidden masses. It is a flow-matching transformer trained only on simple rubble parts.
